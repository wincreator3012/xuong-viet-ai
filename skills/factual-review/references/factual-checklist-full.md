# FACTUAL checklist: Complete reference

This is the complete, detailed checklist for all 7 FACTUAL dimensions. Each sub-check includes what to look for, how to check it, and examples of common issues.

## F - FACTS: Is the information accurate?

### F1. Claims without evidence
- Look for: Statements that sound factual but have no source or citation.
- Example issue: "73% of businesses report increased productivity with this method" - no citation provided.
- How to check: Highlight any statement containing statistics, named studies, or authoritative-sounding claims. Search for corroboration.
- Search tip: Use "site:.edu" or "site:.gov" for authoritative sources.

### F2. Specific facts, dates, and numbers
- Look for: Any specific statistic, date, name, or event claim.
- Example issue: A quote attributed to Richard Feynman that turned out not to be his.
- How to check: Google specific statistics, dates, names, or events. For quotes, use quotation marks in search for exact match.
- Red flags: Very precise numbers without context (e.g., "exactly 27.3% improvement").

### F3. Cross-reference with reliable sources
- Look for: Claims that depend on a single source or no source.
- How to check: Cross-reference with at least 2 independent, credible sources. For academic claims, use Google Scholar instead of regular Google.
- Key insight: Even if AI cites a source, verify the citation actually exists. AI frequently fabricates sources.

### F4. Citation verification
- Look for: Quoted material, attributed statements, referenced studies.
- Example issue: AI incorrectly attributed a quote to Haruki Murakami that was actually spoken by a character (Nagasawa) in his novel Norwegian Wood.
- How to check: Verify that quoted material actually appears in the cited source. Check that the context matches - a character's line is not the author's opinion.
- Pro tip: For scientific claims, check the methodology of studies, not just headlines.

## As - ASSUMPTIONS: What underlying beliefs shape the content?

### As1. Frameworks or worldviews
- Look for: Content that assumes certain values, ideological positions, or theoretical frameworks without acknowledging them.
- Example issue: Content assuming individualistic values over collective ones without recognizing this as a perspective choice.
- How to check: Ask "What cultural or ideological lens shapes this perspective?" and "Would someone from a different framework see this differently?"

### As2. Hidden premises (syllogistic reasoning)
- Look for: Logical jumps where conclusion C does not follow from premise A without an unstated premise B.
- Example issue: "Boss criticizes me → Boss hates me." Hidden premise B: "Criticism equals hate."
- How to check: Identify the stated premise (A) and conclusion (C). Ask: what must be true (B) to connect them? Is B actually true?
- Method: Map A→B→C chains explicitly. The unstated B is where assumptions hide.

### As3. "Common sense" claims
- Look for: Statements presented as universally obvious truth using words like "obviously," "everyone knows," "clearly," "of course."
- Example issue: "Pink is for girls" - actually a post-WWII marketing convention, not a universal truth.
- How to check: Question any claim that relies on "common sense" rather than evidence. Many "obvious" truths are cultural conventions.

### As4. Cultural or contextual assumptions
- Look for: Content that assumes a specific cultural, geographic, or socioeconomic context applies universally.
- Example issue: Marketing advice based on Western consumer behavior applied to Asian markets.
- How to check: Ask "Would this apply equally in different countries, age groups, or socioeconomic contexts?"

## C - COMPLETENESS: Is anything important missing?

### C1. All parts of the question answered
- Look for: Gaps between what was asked and what was provided.
- How to check: For complex questions with multiple parts, number each component and check them off against the response.
- Common issue: AI often answers early parts thoroughly but neglects later points.

### C2. Depth level assessment
- Look for: Whether the depth matches the complexity of the topic, using this progression:
  - Level 1 - Description: What it is (surface facts)
  - Level 2 - Analysis: Why it happens (causes, mechanisms)
  - Level 3 - Prediction: What might happen (implications, trends)
  - Level 4 - Intervention: What to do about it (recommendations, actions)
- How to check: Determine what level the text reaches. Complex topics should go beyond Level 1 Description into Analysis and Intervention.

### C3. Missing context
- Look for: Important factors, conditions, or qualifications left out.
- Example issue: Discussing medication benefits without mentioning side effects.
- How to check: Ask "What would change my understanding if I knew about it?" and "What important context is missing?"

### C4. Missing counterarguments
- Look for: One-sided presentation without opposing viewpoints.
- How to check: Ask "What are the strongest arguments against this position?" If none are mentioned, this is a completeness gap.
- Tip: Prompt the user to ask AI "What are the strongest counterarguments?" as a follow-up.

### C5. Standard information for the field
- Look for: Missing elements that are expected in that type of content.
- Example issue: A marketing plan missing a call-to-action. Financial analysis missing cash flow analysis.
- How to check: Compare content structure against standard frameworks in the relevant domain.

## T - THREATS & BIAS: Is the content balanced and objective?

### T1. Facts vs. opinions
- Look for: Statements that are judgments or evaluations presented as neutral facts.
- Example issue: "Company X had impressive growth" (opinion) vs. "Company X had 10% growth" (fact).
- How to check: Highlight all adjectives in the text. Evaluative adjectives (impressive, unfortunate, excellent, problematic) signal opinions.

### T2. Values disguised as facts
- Look for: Judgments embedded in seemingly neutral language.
- Example issue: "Unfortunately, the policy failed" - "unfortunately" implies a value judgment.
- How to check: Replace evaluative words with neutral ones. If the meaning changes significantly, the original contains hidden values.

### T3. Balanced presentation
- Look for: Whether multiple perspectives receive fair coverage.
- How to check: Count paragraphs or lines devoted to different viewpoints. Quantify the balance.
- Red flag: "10 benefits but only 1 drawback mentioned" signals imbalance.

### T4. Suspiciously confident or precise claims
- Look for: Extremely specific numbers without sources, or absolute certainty about uncertain topics.
- Example issue: "This approach guarantees exactly 27.3% improvement."
- Warning signs: Absolute terms like "always," "never," "guaranteed," "exactly," "proven."

### T5. Claims contradicting established knowledge
- Look for: Information conflicting with well-established scientific or professional consensus.
- Example issue: Health advice contradicting major medical organizations.
- Principle: Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.

## U - UNCERTAINTY: Does it acknowledge limitations in confidence?

### U1. Tentative language
- Look for: Hedging words that signal the text is appropriately cautious about uncertain claims.
- Trigger words: "may," "might," "could," "suggests," "possibly," "preliminary evidence indicates."
- Insight: Hedging when data is limited is a positive sign, not a weakness. Its absence in uncertain domains is a red flag.

### U2. Vague answers to specific questions
- Look for: General, abstract responses when the question asked for specific, concrete information.
- Example issue: AI provides abstract principles when asked for specific implementation steps.
- Insight: Vagueness often signals AI has limited data in that specific area.

### U3. Confidence-evidence mismatch
- Look for: Strong claims with weak support, or tentative language about well-established facts.
- Principle: Strong claims need strong evidence. Tentative claims should match limited evidence.
- Red flag: High confidence with weak or no supporting evidence suggests potential hallucination.

### U4. Consistency under rephrasing
- Look for: Whether the same information holds up when approached from different angles.
- How to check: Compare answers to the same question asked in different ways.
- Pattern: If answers vary significantly with slight rephrasing, the underlying information may be unreliable.

## Ar - ARGUMENTS: Is the reasoning logically sound?

### Ar1. Conclusions follow from evidence
- Look for: Logical connections between stated evidence and conclusions drawn.
- Example issue: A study about "having purpose in life" used to support a claim about "decision-making alignment with inner values" - a logical stretch.
- How to check: For each conclusion, trace it back to specific evidence. If the connection requires unstated steps, the reasoning has a gap.

### Ar2. Missing steps in reasoning
- Look for: Logical jumps or gaps in the argument chain.
- How to check: Use syllogistic analysis. If A leads to C, identify what missing B connects them. Is B stated? Is it valid?
- Common issue: AI often jumps from problem to solution without explaining the causal or logical connection.

### Ar3. Examples support the points
- Look for: Whether illustrative examples actually demonstrate the principle they're meant to support.
- How to check: For each example, explicitly ask "How does this demonstrate the principle claimed?"
- Common issue: AI often provides relevant-sounding but misaligned examples. The topic may be related, but the example doesn't actually prove the specific point.

### Ar4. Correlation vs. causation
- Look for: Claims that one thing causes another based only on co-occurrence.
- Example issue: "Water is found in cancer cells and in dead bodies" does not prove water causes death.
- Caution words: "resulted in," "led to," "because of," "due to" - these imply causation. Check if only correlation was demonstrated.

### Ar5. Alternative explanations
- Look for: Whether the text considers other possible explanations for observations.
- Example issue: Multiple interpretations of why a boss might criticize an employee, but only one is presented.
- How to check: For any conclusion, ask "What else might explain this?" If no alternatives are considered, the reasoning is incomplete.

## L - LIMITATIONS: Are constraints properly recognized?

### L1. Knowledge cutoff / outdated information
- Look for: Information that may have changed since it was written or since AI's training data cutoff.
- How to check: For time-sensitive claims, search for recent sources.
- Tool: Perplexity AI or Google with date filters ("after:YYYY-MM-DD").

### L2. Time-sensitive information
- Look for: Rapidly changing information presented as current.
- High-risk areas: Prices, regulations, statistics, market trends, political positions, technology capabilities.
- How to check: Use date-filtered searches to find the most recent information.

### L3. Need for human judgment
- Look for: Situations requiring ethical reasoning, cultural sensitivity, or contextual understanding that goes beyond factual accuracy.
- Example: Cultural appropriateness, ethical dilemmas, sensitive interpersonal situations.
- Key insight: Some judgments require human values assessment that no verification checklist can substitute.

### L4. Limitations of analysis
- Look for: Missing disclaimers about the scope, data quality, or methodology constraints of any analysis presented.
- How to check: Consider what data or methods were used and what their limitations are.
- Template: "This analysis is based on [scope] and may not apply to [out-of-scope contexts]."

### L5. Need for expert consultation
- Look for: Specialized content that requires professional verification.
- High-risk domains: Medical advice, legal guidance, financial recommendations, engineering specifications, psychological interventions.
- Principle: AI output in these domains should be treated as a starting point for discussion with qualified professionals, never as definitive guidance.

## Cognitive biases to watch for in yourself as reviewer

These biases affect how we evaluate text. Be aware of them during review:

1. **Confirmation bias**: Tendency to accept content that aligns with existing beliefs.
2. **Confidence bias**: Tendency to trust content that sounds authoritative, even if wrong.
3. **Cognitive energy conservation**: Preference for simple explanations over complex ones.
4. **Pattern completion**: Filling in gaps with assumptions based on past experiences.

Counter-measure: Before accepting any claim that confirms your prior view, specifically look for evidence that could contradict it.
